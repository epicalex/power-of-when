An Introduction to Grid Flexibility and Virtual Power Plants

It’s six o’clock. People are home from work and school. Dinner’s cooking, TVs are on, and electric cars are plugging in. Welcome to peak demand.

In Great Britain, electricity demand often peaks in the early evening, especially in winter. Meeting that peak can mean calling on extra generation, including gas-fired ‘peaking plants’.

But could devices already in our homes help meet the peak—by supplying power or shifting when we use it? In this guide, we’ll explore demand flexibility and how coordinated devices can work together as a virtual power plant.

THE PROBLEM

A peak in demand.
More power, right now.

Many of us need electricity at similar times. As cooking, heating and other uses overlap, demand rises. The system must keep supply and demand in balance through that peak.

One traditional response is to call on peaking power stations, often fuelled by gas. They supplement other supply when extra power is needed. They are one of several balancing tools, alongside storage, Links between electricity networks, often cables between countries, that let power be imported or exported. 03 /

YOU MAY ALREADY KNOW THIS IDEA

A small change at home.
A response for the grid.

This already happens outside the experiment. You might have had an email or a notification from your energy supplier offering money or points for using less electricity during a particular hour. Octopus’s Saving Sessions are one example. These are real offers for households to help reduce demand during peak hours, with rewards for changing when they use electricity.

Many of these offers are delivered through Great Britain’s Demand Flexibility Service (DFS), run by NESO, the National Energy System Operator. NESO arranges the response through participating energy suppliers and aggregators—companies that bring together lots of smaller customers. Your provider tells you when to use less electricity, how long to do it for, and how your reward will be calculated.

Imagine the event runs from 18:00 to 19:00. You were going to charge the car as soon as you got home, but it doesn’t need to be ready until tomorrow morning. Waiting until after 19:00 still gets you the same charge, while taking that demand out of the busy hour. Now imagine thousands of other households making a similar change. Together, those small decisions can reduce the amount of extra generation the system needs at that moment. That can reduce the need to turn up or start gas-fired peaking plants.

Some people might simply switch off something they don’t need. Others are moving a job to earlier or later in the day. So using less during the event doesn’t necessarily mean using less electricity overall. For the grid, both avoiding unnecessary use and moving flexible tasks can help reduce the peak.

  1. 01 / THE INVITATION

    Your provider sets a window.

    It tells you when to respond and how rewards work. You choose whether to take part.

  2. 02 / THE ACTION

    You move a flexible task.

    For example, delay an EV charge while keeping the car ready for its next journey.

  3. 03 / THE MEASUREMENT

    The change is assessed.

    Smart-meter readings are compared with an estimated baseline: what you would otherwise have used. Rewards follow your provider’s rules.

FROM A ROUTINE TO AUTOMATION

What if you could set the result you need, and let your devices manage the timing?

Choosing to wait an hour is a fairly simple change. But doing that every time means noticing the message, deciding what can move, and remembering to do it later. That’s where automation can help.

Think back to the car in our experiment. What you actually need is enough charge by the time you leave. You could tell a smart charger, “I need the car ready by 07:00,” and let it work out when to charge. It could respond to a cheaper tariff, a period of strong renewable generation, or a request to help the grid, while keeping that deadline in place.

The same idea applies to other equipment. You might let a heat pump adjust its timing within a comfortable temperature range, or let a battery discharge while keeping some energy in reserve. You decide what matters to you; the software works within those limits, with a way for you to take back control when your plans change.

So far, we’ve been talking about one household letting its devices manage the timing. Now imagine doing that across a whole neighbourhood, or thousands of homes across the country. Each household still sets its own limits, but a shared system can work out how those different pieces fit together. One car might need to charge now; another can wait. One battery might have energy to spare while another needs to keep it in reserve.

At that scale, the result is more than lots of separate smart homes. The devices can be coordinated and offered to the electricity system as one resource: a virtual power plant, or VPP. Its operator forecasts what the fleet can deliver, schedules the devices and measures the response. Rather than asking a single power station for more output, the grid can ask an aggregator to deliver a response from many smaller devices.

DFS is one service that can buy that flexibility. The VPP is what brings the devices together to deliver it, whether that means helping during a particular event or managing charging, heating and storage through the day. The next step is to look at what those devices can actually contribute.

AUTOMATE THE TIMING

The resources may
already be here.

The same idea extends beyond a single task. Look around a neighbourhood: home batteries, plugged-in EVs, heat pumps and rooftop solar. Each has a different role. Together, at sufficient scale, they can provide some of the services we currently ask of a power station.

Each resource brings a different kind of flexibility. A battery can supply stored electricity; a car can delay charging; a warm building can briefly reduce heating. The software works within their owners’ limits. It doesn’t create energy; it changes when energy is supplied, stored or used. We’re focusing on homes here, but businesses and larger generators can be part of a VPP too.

WHEN DOES IT BECOME A POWER PLANT?

Could the operator
rely on it?

Meeting one event is a start. A power system also needs to know what a resource can do, schedule it, and rely on it being available.

The Huels test, named after Matthias Huels and proposed by EnergyHub, asks whether a VPP could substitute for a conventional peaking power plant without the grid operator noticing a difference in the service delivered. It is EnergyHub’s proposed operational benchmark.

01 / VISIBILITY

Can I see it?

Accurate, timely measurements sent to the operator, showing what the fleet is doing and what it can deliver.

02 / SCHEDULABILITY

Can I direct it?

Follow a requested profile, including ramps, duration and recovery.

03 / AVAILABILITY

Will it be there?

Deliver for several hours across different seasons, with clear forecasts of when the fleet can help.

A proposed path to maturity

EnergyHub’s framework places passing the Huels test at Level 3; the levels describe progressively broader capabilities.

  1. LEVEL 0Basic demand response

    Activate a response with little performance visibility.

  2. LEVEL 1Response with data

    Connected devices exchange commands and performance data.

  3. LEVEL 2Enhanced demand response

    Shape delivery across seasons, with some control over where the response occurs.

  4. LEVEL 3 / HUELS TESTAutomated VPP

    Predictable delivery integrated into grid operations.

  5. LEVEL 4Grid-adaptive VPP

    Coordinate customer, local-network and wider-system needs.

↳

Dependability takes more than a good curve. Dependable operation needs timely operating data, sustained availability across seasons and integration with grid-control systems. Earlier forms of demand response can still provide substantial value.

ABOUT THIS EXPLAINER

A note on the models.

The experiments use simplified, invented demand, weather, device and tariff profiles to explain how flexibility works. Outputs are educational examples, not measured grid performance, forecasts, energy offers or guaranteed savings. Historical programme results are separately attributed to their sources.

The models omit many real operating, network and market constraints. Charging and dispatch assume ideal information and control; costs exclude standing charges, fees, export payments and charging losses. A simulated response does not establish that a real VPP can deliver it or pass the Huels test, which is a proposed benchmark rather than certification.

Model details, assumptions and supporting sources

Renewable abundance and vehicle-to-grid

The demand-turn-up example uses ten EVs, each needing 14 kWh at 7 kW over two hours. All are assumed available at 12:00 and 18:00; the slider moves whole cars between these windows. Other demand and solar availability stay fixed. “Unused solar” is the positive difference between the shown solar supply and demand, with no storage, exports or other flexible loads in this small system. It illustrates potential curtailment, not a national forecast or a calculation of DFS payments.

Octopus Power Pack gives a real example of vehicle-to-grid with compatible cars and bidirectional chargers. The EV models on this page do not include discharge.

About peakers and this example

This invented system has up to 300 MW available from all sources other than the peaker; its demand profile and peaker output are illustrative, not measured UK data. The invented curve has an evening peak, a lower overnight period and a morning rise. Actual patterns vary with season, weather and embedded solar generation; see NESO’s daily demand forecast definitions. The other supply is used only as needed. The example omits network constraints and detailed generator operation. GAO’s overview of peak-demand power plants explains the peaking role using US evidence; no US fleet statistics are applied to this example.

How to read the renewable and cost results

The dashed amber curve is an invented share of wider wind and solar generation, not just this home’s rooftop solar. The hatched coral gap is demand not covered by these renewables. The rest of the grid supplies it: it is not an outage or a limit on the renewables the home could actually use. The renewable percentage counts only EV charging matched in the same time interval, after other household use; unused renewable supply is not carried forward. It does not measure the actual source of electricity reaching the home or its carbon emissions. Charging can start from noon here: assume the car is home and plugged in.

Costs sum power × 15 minutes × the tariff rate in each interval. Household totals cover noon to noon. All demand is bought at this tariff: the shown renewables are a share of wider generation, not free electricity or a discount. Weather changes the renewable overlap, not these prices. Standing charges, export payments and charging losses are excluded.

Behind this experiment

We hold charging energy at 14 kWh. Charging power is constant within the chosen window: 14 divided by its duration, from 7 kW over two hours to about 1.17 kW over twelve. The comparison stays fixed at the original 18:00, two-hour charge. Costs integrate all 15-minute power intervals using the invented tariff: 10p/kWh from 00:00 to 06:00, 35p from 16:00 to 21:00 and 25p otherwise; the end of each period is exclusive. The renewable allocation does not reduce metered purchases in this example. This is ideal continuous power modulation, without minimum-current limits or charging losses. The window stays within noon today and your departure deadline, assuming the car is available throughout. Other household use is held constant when charging moves. Wind and solar patterns are illustrative scenarios, and the chart scale stays at 0–10 kW. The renewable-gap metric sums max(total demand − allocated renewable power, 0) across 15-minute intervals; unused allocation is not banked for later. The home and renewable profiles are invented to explain timing; they are not measured UK data. Real charging includes losses and may vary in power. NESO: introduction to flexibility ↗

DFS sources and scope

This introduction describes Great Britain’s service, not a universal programme or a guaranteed reward. Participation and payments depend on provider terms. See NESO: DFS explained and NESO’s August 2026 service update. The Octopus figures above describe winter 2022–23, not today’s service design.

What this coordination example assumes

This separate invented street has one EV per EV-owning home, 7 kW maximum chargers, arrivals from 16:00–20:00, departures from 05:00–09:00 and requirements from 7–17.5 kWh. Other household use is fixed. The local network limit applies to total power, not just EVs; this is an aggregate capacity illustration, not a network power-flow calculation. It is independent of the household and event experiments.

“On arrival” starts each car immediately. “Everyone at midnight” starts at midnight regardless of the shared limit. “Coordinate charging” gives earlier departures priority and allocates lower-priced slots first, preferring less-loaded slots at equal prices, while respecting individual availability and available capacity. It uses the same 10p/25p/35p tariff as the household example. This simple scheduling rule has perfect advance information and ideal power modulation; it is not a globally optimal or real-time VPP controller. It may leave energy undelivered when capacity is insufficient. Curves above the local limit show requested demand, not physically feasible delivery. Readiness counts completed schedules, not a guarantee of supply when capacity is exceeded. No strategy claims a missed target is a completed journey; partial-delivery costs are labelled.

How this neighbourhood works — and where it’s simplified

40% of homes have an EV, 30% a 10 kWh battery, 50% a heat pump and 40% a 3 kW rooftop solar installation; ownership can overlap. Participation is rounded within each group. Cars need 14 kWh by 07:00 and usually start charging between 18:00 and 18:45. During evening events, enrolled cars can reduce their scheduled charging and make up the deferred energy afterwards. In the solar scenario, only one in five enrolled cars is assumed to be home during the afternoon. EV charging can modulate continuously between 0 and 7 kW in this simplified model; real chargers have minimum current settings and other constraints.

Batteries start at 80%, with 3 kW event power, 2 kW recovery power and 95% efficiency in each direction. Their initial charge is restored by the end of the modelled day. The room model uses 4 kWh/°C thermal capacity, 0.32 kW/°C heat loss, constant heat-pump COP 3 and maximum electrical heating power 3.5 kW. Outdoors is 8°C, or 0°C on the cold day. The solar event can preheat rooms to 21°C; evening events respect your selected minimum.

The on-ramp admits two cohorts over 30 minutes or four over 60 minutes, at 15-minute resolution. During each interval the requested power is the same fraction of the full target as the fraction of cohorts admitted. This is a stepped approximation, not a continuous or second-by-second ramp. “Activate everything” uses all available flexibility from admitted cohorts. “Follow the request” reassesses device states every 15 minutes and allocates the requested power, using heating flexibility first, then EV charging and batteries. It preserves the temperature floor, battery reserve and charging deadlines. This simple priority rule is illustrative, not a cost-optimal dispatch algorithm. Both modes use the same optional staggered recovery afterwards. “On target” counts event intervals within ±5% of that interval’s requested kW; both shortfalls and excessive delivery fall outside that band. This teaching tolerance is not a market requirement. The model has ideal device-state information, no forecast error and no communication delay. It cannot demonstrate real-time or second-by-second grid services. “Without the event” is a known simulated comparison, not a real market baseline. Real baselines must be estimated and verified.

Solar output follows an invented daylight curve, scaled to 40% on the cold-day scenario. It reduces both comparison curves equally and is zero at night. It is neither added to the controllable response nor commanded to generate. The chart models aggregate power, not electrical network flows or frequency. It does not represent local cable constraints, market qualification, forecast errors or outages. A data connection is not an electrical wire. Batteries can export; ordinary EV chargers here cannot. Heating changes can change total energy; battery losses can increase it. These profiles are educational assumptions, not measured outcomes or a prediction.
