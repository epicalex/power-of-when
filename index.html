THE PROBLEM

A peak in demand.
More power, right now.

Many of us need electricity at similar times. As cooking, heating and other uses overlap, demand rises. The system must keep supply and demand in balance through that peak.

One traditional response is to call on peaking power stations, often fuelled by gas. They supplement other supply when extra power is needed. They are one of several balancing tools, alongside storage, Links between electricity networks, often cables between countries, that let power be imported or exported. Imports can help meet demand, within the link’s available capacity. and flexible demand.

02 /

A DIFFERENT KIND OF POWER PLANT

The resources may
already be here.

Look around a neighbourhood: home batteries, plugged-in EVs, heat pumps and rooftop solar. Each has a different role. Together, at sufficient scale, they can provide some of the services we ask of a power station.

Software brings those resources together as a virtual power plant, or VPP. It coordinates real devices, within their owners’ limits. It doesn’t create energy; it changes when energy is supplied, stored or used.

  • ▤

    Batteries supply stored energy.

    Charge earlier, then discharge when extra power is needed.

  • ϟ

    EV charging can wait.

    Delay some demand while keeping the car ready for its next journey. These chargers do not export.

  • ≋

    Warm buildings buy some time.

    Adjust heat-pump use while staying inside an agreed comfort range.

  • ☼

    Solar supplies daytime electricity.

    Its output depends on daylight and weather. It cannot be turned up at will after sunset.

THE IDEA

Sometimes we can change when we use electricity without changing what we get from it. That room to move is flexibility.

WHY TIMING MATTERS

The weather doesn’t
follow our timetable.

Electricity supply and demand must stay in balance. Wind and solar change with the weather; our needs change throughout the day. Moving demand can help bring the two closer.

And it works both ways. On a sunny afternoon, using more at the right time can help make use of renewable electricity.

How this neighbourhood works — and where it’s simplified

40% of homes have an EV, 30% a 10 kWh battery, 50% a heat pump and 40% a 3 kW rooftop solar installation; ownership can overlap. Participation is rounded within each group. Cars need 14 kWh by 07:00 and usually start charging between 18:00 and 18:45. During evening events, enrolled cars can reduce their scheduled charging and make up the deferred energy afterwards. In the solar scenario, only one in five enrolled cars is assumed to be home during the afternoon. EV charging can modulate continuously between 0 and 7 kW in this simplified model; real chargers have minimum current settings and other constraints.

Batteries start at 80%, with 3 kW event power, 2 kW recovery power and 95% efficiency in each direction. Their initial charge is restored by the end of the modelled day. The room model uses 4 kWh/°C thermal capacity, 0.32 kW/°C heat loss, constant heat-pump COP 3 and maximum electrical heating power 3.5 kW. Outdoors is 8°C, or 0°C on the cold day. The solar event can preheat rooms to 21°C; evening events respect your selected minimum.

The on-ramp admits two cohorts over 30 minutes or four over 60 minutes, at 15-minute resolution. During each interval the requested power is the same fraction of the full target as the fraction of cohorts admitted. This is a stepped approximation, not a continuous or second-by-second ramp. “Activate everything” uses all available flexibility from admitted cohorts. “Follow the request” reassesses device states every 15 minutes and allocates the requested power, using heating flexibility first, then EV charging and batteries. It preserves the temperature floor, battery reserve and charging deadlines. This simple priority rule is illustrative, not a cost-optimal dispatch algorithm. Both modes use the same optional staggered recovery afterwards. “On target” counts event intervals within ±5% of that interval’s requested kW; both shortfalls and excessive delivery fall outside that band. This teaching tolerance is not a market requirement. The model has ideal device-state information, no forecast error and no communication delay. It cannot demonstrate real-time or second-by-second grid services. “Without the event” is a known simulated comparison, not a real market baseline. Real baselines must be estimated and verified.

Solar output follows an invented daylight curve, scaled to 40% on the cold-day scenario. It reduces both comparison curves equally and is zero at night. It is neither added to the controllable response nor commanded to generate. The chart models aggregate power, not electrical network flows or frequency. It does not represent local cable constraints, market qualification, forecast errors or outages. A data connection is not an electrical wire. Batteries can export; ordinary EV chargers here cannot. Heating changes can change total energy; battery losses can increase it. These profiles are educational assumptions, not measured outcomes or a prediction.

WHEN DOES IT BECOME A POWER PLANT?

Could the operator
rely on it?

Meeting one event is a start. A power system also needs to know what a resource can do, schedule it, and rely on it being available.

The Huels test, named after Matthias Huels and proposed by EnergyHub, asks whether a VPP could substitute for a conventional power plant without the grid operator noticing a difference in the service delivered. It is a proposed benchmark, not a formal certification.

01 / VISIBILITY

Can I see it?

Useful measurements of what the fleet is doing and what it can deliver.

02 / SCHEDULABILITY

Can I direct it?

Follow a requested profile, including ramps, duration and recovery.

03 / AVAILABILITY

Will it be there?

Know when the fleet can help and the limits on its response.

A proposed path to maturity

EnergyHub’s framework places passing the Huels test at Level 3; the levels describe capabilities, not a score awarded by this page.

  1. LEVEL 0Basic demand response

    Activate a response with little performance visibility.

  2. LEVEL 1Response with data

    Connected devices report what happened.

  3. LEVEL 2Enhanced demand response

    Shape delivery across more conditions and seasons.

  4. LEVEL 3 / HUELS TESTAutomated VPP

    Predictable delivery integrated into grid operations.

  5. LEVEL 4Grid-adaptive VPP

    Coordinate customer, local-network and wider-system needs.

↳

A good-looking curve does not pass the test. Our model assumes perfect information and uses 15-minute steps. It demonstrates scheduling ideas, not real telemetry, dependable year-round availability or integration with grid-control systems. Earlier forms of demand response can still provide substantial value.
